How it works

The ridge is how much electricity all of New York is expected to use each hour of that day. It comes from the forecast that the state's grid operator, NYISO, publishes every morning.

The peaker line. On most days, demand climbs every evening. Once it gets high enough, the grid has to switch on its last and most expensive power plants, called peakers. Many of them are older gas and oil units that sit near the homes they serve. Kilowhen draws the line halfway between the busiest hour that isn't a peak hour and the quietest hour that is. A peak hour is one whose price is in the day's top quarter, at least 25% above the day's middle price, and whose demand is within 8% of the day's highest. Everything above the line is amber.

Smokestacks. Each stack stands for one peaker unit of 40 MW, a common size for the small turbines that run at peak. The number lit is the amber power at the playhead's hour divided by 40, rounded up. This is a picture of the idea, not a map of real plants.

Prices. The cost on each appliance uses NYISO's day-ahead price for the zone you pick, in dollars per megawatt-hour, which is what the energy itself costs on the wholesale market. Your bill adds delivery and taxes on top.

Homes doing what you did. Each step takes a share of the 261,372 households in New York's 17th congressional district, from the U.S. Census, and assumes those homes ran the same appliances at the same usual times and moved them to the times you chose. The ridge redraws with that power taken out of the old hours and added to the new ones.

Stagger the homes. If thousands of homes all move to the same cheap hour, they create a new peak there. Staggering spreads the same energy into the lowest-demand hours that aren't peak hours, filling the overnight valley first, the way a smart charging program would.

Updates. A scheduled job copies NYISO's newest files every 30 minutes. Nothing about you is collected or sent anywhere.

Data: NYISO, U.S. Census Bureau.
