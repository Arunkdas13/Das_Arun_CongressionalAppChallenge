How it works

Kilowhen uses public grid data and fixed, readable formulas. There is no AI, no account and no tracking.

Where the numbers come from

For New York, Kilowhen reads three public files from the New York Independent System Operator (NYISO), which runs the state's power grid. The day-ahead price is the wholesale price of electricity for each hour of the next day, set every morning for each of New York's 11 zones. The load forecast is NYISO's prediction of how much power each zone will use each hour. The fuel mix is what is actually generating power right now, by fuel, every five minutes. A scheduled job copies the newest files every 30 minutes.

For other U.S. regions, Kilowhen uses the federal Hourly Electric Grid Monitor from the U.S. Energy Information Administration. Those regions have no free hourly price, so Kilowhen times your loads by expected pollution instead, using each hour's average pounds of CO2 per megawatt-hour over the past week.

Peak hours

An hour counts as a peak hour when its price is in the most expensive quarter of the day, at least 25% above the day's median, and, when a load forecast exists, demand is within 8% of the day's highest. These are the hours when the grid has to call on its last and most expensive power plants. In New York those are often older gas and oil units called peakers, many of them near the homes they serve.

The best time to run something

For each appliance, Kilowhen looks at every possible start time between when you would normally start it and when it has to be done. It adds up the price of every hour the appliance would run, then picks the start with the lowest total. Savings are shown at wholesale prices, which is what the energy itself costs the grid. Your bill adds delivery charges and taxes on top. If you are on a time-of-use plan, enter your rates on the Plan page to see your actual savings.

Pollution

When generation data is available, each hour gets an estimated emissions rate: the mix of fuels running that hour multiplied by typical pounds of CO2 per megawatt-hour for each fuel. Kilowhen uses approximately 900 for natural gas, 1,150 for New York's dual-fuel units, 1,700 for oil, 2,200 for coal, and zero for nuclear, hydro, wind and solar. These are estimates. Actual plants vary.

If we all did it

This multiplies the number of households in a place, from the U.S. Census, by the share of homes taking part and by how much power each home moves out of peak hours. It compares the result with the size of a typical peaker unit and with the area's forecast peak. Every input can be changed. It is a what-if, not a prediction.

Limits

Prices are the day-ahead market, which can differ from real-time prices. Zones do not line up exactly with towns or congressional districts. The peak-hour rule is a simple, transparent test, not NYISO's own dispatch.
